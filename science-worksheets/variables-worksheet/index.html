Variables Worksheets

Independent, dependent, controlled — three words that students can define perfectly and still get wrong the moment they are looking at an actual experiment. These sheets give space to sort them out for a specific investigation: what you deliberately change, what you measure as a result, and the list of things you must deliberately keep the same for the comparison to mean anything. That last column is the one most students leave short, and it is usually the reason an experiment does not prove what they hoped.

Worksheets with labeled areas for the independent variable, the dependent variable and a multi-line list for controlled variables, so a single experiment can be broken down in one place. Some layouts include blank rows for identifying variables in several short described experiments. Ruled writing space, black and white, one sheet per US Letter page.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between independent and dependent variables?
The independent variable is the one you choose to change — the amount of water, the type of surface, the temperature setting. The dependent variable is what you measure to see the effect — the height, the distance, the time taken. A useful check: the dependent variable depends on the independent one, never the other way round.
Why do controlled variables matter so much?
Because without them you cannot say the independent variable caused anything. If one plant gets more water and also sits on a sunnier windowsill, the extra growth has two possible causes and the experiment cannot separate them. Listing controlled variables in advance is how you spot that before running the test.
Are controlled variables the same as a control group?
No, and the similar names cause endless confusion. Controlled variables are the conditions you keep identical across every trial. A control group is a whole extra trial that receives no treatment, used as a baseline for comparison. An experiment can have both.
What grade is this suitable for?
As an editorial suggestion, roughly ages 9 and up. Younger students can usually identify what was changed and what was measured; the controlled variables column is the part that develops later and benefits most from being written out.
