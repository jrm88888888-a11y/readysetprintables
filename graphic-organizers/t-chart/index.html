The T-chart is the simplest organizer there is and probably the most used: two columns, two headings, one comparison. Pros and cons, facts and opinions, before and after, what I know and what I want to know. Its power is entirely in the constraint — two columns force a genuine pairing rather than a list that drifts. Printed versions beat drawing one on the board because the header boxes are already there, which quietly reminds students to name what each column contains before they start filling it.

Blank T-charts with two equal columns and a boxed header at the top of each, plus ruled writing lines down both sides so entries stay aligned across the divide. Variants include a wide-line version for younger writers and a narrow-line version that fits more entries. Black and white, one chart per US Letter page.

Frequently asked questions

What can a T-chart be used for?
Anything with two sides: advantages and disadvantages, causes and effects, facts and opinions, problem and solution, then and now, evidence for and against a claim. It also works for vocabulary — word on one side, definition or example on the other.
How is a T-chart different from a compare and contrast organizer?
A T-chart lists two things side by side without formally identifying what they share. A compare and contrast organizer, or a Venn diagram, has dedicated space for similarities. Use a T-chart when the two columns are genuinely opposed, and a Venn when overlap is the interesting part.
Should entries line up across the two columns?
It depends on the task. For pros and cons they usually should not — you rarely have a matching drawback for every benefit. For before and after, or word and definition, aligned rows are much easier to read, which is why these templates rule both columns to the same line spacing.
Is a T-chart too simple for older students?
No — the format scales with the thinking that goes into it. As an editorial suggestion it works from around age 6 with an adult naming the columns, and it stays useful for adults weighing a decision. What changes with age is the sophistication of what goes in each column, not the tool.
