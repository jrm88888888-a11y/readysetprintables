A blank number bond is one of the most reusable things in an early math classroom. The same empty diagram covers finding pairs that make ten, splitting a number for mental addition, showing a fact family, and later on partitioning for column methods. Because it is blank, you set the difficulty: write 10 in the whole and let a child find the parts, or write both parts and ask for the whole. One printed sheet outlasts a dozen pre-filled worksheets.

Ten different one-page templates, US Letter, black and white: six bonds per page; nine per page; four large bonds with room for counters; a single part-part-whole mat; six three-part bonds (a whole split into three parts); bonds with the whole at the bottom; sideways bonds with the whole on the left; four bonds each with lines for two addition and two subtraction facts; and make-10 and make-20 sheets with the whole filled in and both parts blank. They are templates, so there is nothing to mark and no answer page.

Frequently asked questions

What is a number bond?
A diagram showing how a number splits into parts — a whole in one circle joined to two or more parts. Seven splits into three and four, so 3 and 4 are a number bond to 7. It shows addition and subtraction as the same relationship viewed two ways.
How do number bonds help with mental math?
They make partitioning explicit. A child adding 8 and 5 who knows that 5 splits into 2 and 3 can make 8 and 2 into ten and then add the remaining 3. That bridging-through-ten strategy is built directly on number bond fluency.
What is the difference between a number bond and a fact family?
A fact family is the set of four equations you can write from the same three numbers — two additions and two subtractions. A number bond is the diagram those equations come from. Many teachers use one to introduce the other, with the bond drawn first and the four facts written underneath.
What age are these for?
As an editorial guide, roughly ages 5 to 8. Start with the part-part-whole mat or the large bonds and counters, move to the make-10 sheet, then make-20 and the fact-family sheet. Because the other sheets are blank, you set the numbers, so the same template works at a harder level later.
Why do the bonds point in different directions?
Many maths-mastery schemes deliberately vary the layout, so that a child identifies the whole by what the diagram shows rather than by which circle happens to be on top. The whole-at-the-bottom and sideways sheets give that variation; the three-part sheet shows that a whole can split into more than two parts.
