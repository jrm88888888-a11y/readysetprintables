This is the largest shikaku size on the site and the one that rewards patience over speed. A hundred and forty-four cells means the easy forced placements run out early, and the middle of the puzzle usually has to be unpicked through elimination — proving where a rectangle cannot go rather than where it can. If you have been solving 10x10 grids on autopilot, these will slow you down in a good way. Set aside half an hour and use a pencil.

One 12x12 shikaku per page, printed edge to edge so the cells stay large enough to work in despite the grid size. Clue numbers are set in a clear sans-serif that stays readable at small sizes. Frequently asked questions

How long does a 12x12 shikaku take?
For a solver comfortable with 10x10 grids, usually twenty-five to forty minutes. The opening moves go quickly, then there is typically a long middle stretch where progress comes one rectangle at a time, followed by a fast finish once the board is mostly tiled.
I am stuck with several possible layouts. What now?
Stop placing and start eliminating. Pick a single empty cell and list every clue that could legally reach it. If only one can, that clue's rectangle must include that cell, and its shape is often forced from there. This cell-first approach breaks deadlocks that clue-first scanning misses.
Do I need to fill in every cell?
Yes. A finished shikaku leaves no cell outside a rectangle. If you reach the end with a gap, something earlier is wrong — the gap is usually adjacent to an oversized rectangle you drew a step too far.
Are these suitable for older students?
As an editorial suggestion, they suit strong solvers from roughly age 12 up, and they work well as an adult puzzle. Younger children usually do better on the 5x5 and 7x7 sets, which reach a satisfying finish much sooner.
